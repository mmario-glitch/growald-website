ACoS alone is not enough: Which metrics really explain Amazon growth

A low ACoS can be a good signal. But it can just as well hide that revenue, organic visibility, or contribution margin are stagnating. What matters is how they work together.

August 28, 2026 · 8 min read · Mario Moosbauer

ACoS is one of the best-known metrics in Amazon Advertising. It puts ad spend in relation to ad-attributed revenue. That answers an important but narrow question: How much ad budget was spent for each euro of revenue that Amazon attributes to an ad?

ACoS = ad spend ÷ ad-attributed revenue × 100ROAS = ad-attributed revenue ÷ ad spend

Amazon itself points out that there is no universally "good" ACoS. The target value depends, among other things, on margin, campaign goal, and growth phase. That is exactly why a single percentage is not enough to manage an account.

Why a low ACoS can even be a warning sign

Imagine a campaign that consistently cuts bids down to keywords that already convert very efficiently. ACoS drops. At the same time, impressions, new customers, and total revenue decline. The dashboard looks better, the business gets smaller.

A second example: A bestseller has strong conversion and a low ACoS but is regularly out of stock. Advertising speeds up the sell-through, after which the listing loses its sales history and organic rankings. The campaign numbers were good; the overall decision was bad.

So the right question is not: "How do we lower ACoS?" It is: "What role should advertising play for revenue, margin, visibility, and inventory?"

The metrics system for reliable Amazon growth

1. Break-even ACoS: What does the product margin allow?

In simplified terms, break-even ACoS marks the limit up to which advertising does not create a loss before further overhead. The starting point is not the selling price but the amount left after VAT, Amazon fees, purchasing, freight, packaging, returns, and other variable costs.

Rule of thumb: An ACoS of 25% can be healthy at a 45% contribution margin and unprofitable at an 18% contribution margin. Without a product-level cost basis, the assessment is incomplete.

2. TACoS: How much total revenue does advertising support?

TACoS puts ad spend in relation to total Amazon revenue, paid and organic. It shows how strongly the overall business depends on advertising.

TACoS = ad spend ÷ total revenue × 100

If TACoS falls while total revenue grows, that often suggests organic sales are getting stronger. If it rises, it can be a deliberate launch investment, or a sign that organic demand and conversion are not growing along with it.

3. Organic revenue share: Is lasting demand being built?

Advertising shouldn't just buy individual clicks. For many products, it should open up relevant search terms, trigger sales, and thereby support a stronger organic foundation. That's why we watch the share of organic sales as well as the development of strategic keyword positions.

Important: Attribution is not proof of causality. A higher organic share can also come from season, price, reviews, or external demand. It has to be read in context.

4. Conversion rate: Is the detail page ready for more traffic?

If clicks come but purchases don't, targeting is not automatically the problem. Price, main image, reviews, delivery time, variants, bullet points, and A+ Content all influence the purchase decision. More budget then only amplifies a weak product detail page.

Conversion should be tied to price changes, content updates, review trends, and availability at the ASIN and time-period level.

5. Cost per click and search term quality: Where does inefficiency arise?

ACoS can rise because click prices increase, because conversion falls, or because the advertised search terms become less relevant. These causes call for different measures. Cutting bids across the board solves only one of them.

  • CPC rises: Check placements, bids, competition, and match types.
  • Conversion falls: Check retail readiness, price, content, reviews, and delivery time.
  • Relevance falls: Check search terms, negatives, and campaign structure.

6. Availability: Can the product range even absorb growth?

Inventory is a marketing metric. A product with seven days of coverage must not be scaled like a product with 90 days of coverage. Forecast, replenishment lead time, and safety stock therefore have to feed into budget decisions.

We use at least three states: scale, stabilize, and protect inventory. That way, campaigns don't only react once the ASIN is already no longer purchasable.

7. Contribution margin after advertising: What is really left?

Revenue is not profit and ROAS is not ROI. The key management figure is contribution margin after variable costs and advertising. Ideally it is evaluated per ASIN, marketplace, and month.

CM after ads = net revenue − product costs − Amazon fees − logistics − returns − advertising costs

A dashboard that triggers decisions

Good reporting doesn't show as many numbers as possible. It connects metrics with a decision. For every prioritized ASIN, at least the following values should be visible:

  • Total revenue, ad revenue, and organic revenue share
  • Ad spend, ACoS, TACoS, and ROAS
  • Contribution margin before and after advertising
  • Impressions, CTR, CPC, and conversion rate
  • Inventory, coverage, and expected incoming stock
  • Price, coupon, reviews, and relevant content changes

In addition, every ASIN needs a short comment: What happened? What is the most likely cause? What are we changing? What target value do we expect by when?

A sensible management rhythm

Not every metric needs the same frequency. Search terms, budget limits, and outliers can be monitored weekly or automatically. Profitability, organic share, and product range effects call for more of a monthly and quarterly view.

  1. Daily: Budget outages, strong anomalies, Buy Box, and critical inventory.
  2. Weekly: Search terms, bids, placements, conversion outliers, and campaign hygiene.
  3. Monthly: TACoS, contribution margin, organic share, profitability per ASIN, and tests.
  4. Quarterly: Product role in the range, launch pipeline, countries, pricing strategy, and capital tied up.

A worked example: Break-even ACoS in three steps

Break-even ACoS is the most important number in this system, and also the one that is least often calculated cleanly. Let's work through it with a concrete product.

Starting point: A product sells for €29.90 gross, which is €25.13 net at 19% VAT.

  • Unit cost including freight and customs (landed cost): €7.80
  • Amazon referral fee, 15% of the gross price: €4.49
  • FBA fulfillment fee: €3.45
  • Prorated storage costs: €0.40
  • Return costs at a 4% return rate: €0.55

Step 1: Contribution margin before advertising: €25.13 − €16.69 total costs = €8.44.

Step 2: Margin in percent: €8.44 divided by €25.13 net revenue = 33.6%.

Step 3: Break-even ACoS: It equals exactly this margin. At an ACoS of 33.6%, the contribution margin is fully consumed by advertising, so the product sells but earns nothing.

That changes the assessment fundamentally. An ACoS of 25% is profitable for this product and leaves headroom. For a product with an 18% margin, the same 25% ACoS would be a sure loss. If you set a single target ACoS across all products in the account, you systematically subsidize the low-margin items with the high-margin ones, without noticing.

Rule of thumb: Calculate the break-even ACoS for your five top-selling products individually. Our break-even ACoS calculator does it in two minutes. In most accounts the values fall between 20% and 45%, a spread that makes a blanket target impossible.

A typical failure path and where it shows up

The seven metrics only show their value in combination. Here is an example that repeats regularly in ad accounts:

A team is told to lower ACoS. It reduces bids and pauses the most expensive search terms. Within two weeks ACoS drops from 30% to 22%, and the measure is considered a success.

What happens at the same time, nobody sees, because nobody measures it: the paused search terms were the ones through which new customers found the product. Advertised revenue drops as planned. Organic revenue follows four to six weeks later, because sales velocity and with it the organic ranking decline. TACoS stays constant at first and then rises, because total revenue falls.

After three months the account sits at an excellent ACoS of 21% and a much lower total revenue. The metric that was being managed looks better than before. The business does not.

This pattern is only visible if the organic share and TACoS are tracked alongside. That is exactly why a single ad metric is not enough as a steering figure: it is too easy to optimize without the result improving.

Putting seasonality and product phases in context

A target that applies all year ignores two realities: products go through phases, and markets move with the seasons.

In the launch phase, an ACoS above the break-even point is a deliberate investment in visibility and first reviews. What matters is that this phase is limited in time and budget, for example to eight weeks or a defined amount, and that you decide beforehand which results justify moving to the next phase.

In the growth phase, the break-even ACoS serves as the upper limit, with deliberate room above it for search terms of high strategic value.

In the maturity phase, the target sits well below the break-even point, because a substantial part of demand arises organically and advertising mainly defends shelf space.

Seasonally, click prices rise in peak periods such as the fourth quarter, while the conversion rate rises at the same time. The two effects partly cancel each other out. If you force the same ACoS target in November as in March, you lose visibility exactly when it is worth the most. It makes more sense to review targets each quarter and base them on the contribution margin after advertising rather than on a fixed percentage.

Conclusion: ACoS is an instrument, not a business goal

ACoS remains useful if its limits are understood. It measures the efficiency of attributed ad revenue. Whether a product grows profitably is only explained by the interplay of margin, TACoS, organic share, conversion, availability and contribution margin.

If you manage these levels together, you can decide deliberately when efficiency, launch, market share or inventory protection takes priority. That is what produces an Amazon system that looks good beyond the ad account.
